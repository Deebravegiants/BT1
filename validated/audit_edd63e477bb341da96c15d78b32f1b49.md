### Title
Emporium can drain any native-ETH balance it holds to an attacker-chosen endpoint by simply omitting `address(0)` from `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` - ([File: contracts/external-actions/emporium/upgradeable/EmporiumUpgradeable.sol])

### Summary
`EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction` executes attacker-supplied `EmporiumOperation.value` transfers of native ETH out of the Emporium contract (`op.endpoint.call{value: op.value}(op.callData)`), but only validates that the Emporium contract's balance did not shrink for the tokens present in the fully attacker-controlled `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` array. Because the attacker chooses that array themselves and can simply exclude the native-ETH placeholder `address(0)`, any ETH sitting in Emporium (from earlier legitimate `EmporiumOperation`s or direct sends via `emporium.call{value:...}()`) can be exfiltrated for free to any endpoint the attacker controls, with no accounting check catching the outflow.

### Finding Description
The equality the protocol relies on is: **for every token `i` that changed hands, `balancesAfter[i] - balancesBefore[i]` must equal what the shielded proof authorized (`-deltaAmountChanges[i]`)** — enforced in `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction`: [1](#0-0) [2](#0-1) 

That equality is only computed over `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` (via `getBalancesForArray`), and this array is entirely attacker-supplied as part of `CircomData` — it is only checked for *length* consistency against `dimensions.tokenNumber` in `dimensionsCheck`, never for *contents* (e.g. that it must include every token actually touched by the ops), in `HinkalHelper.dimensionsCheck`: [3](#0-2) 

The same scoping problem exists one layer up in `Hinkal.transact`, which also only measures balance deltas over `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses`: [4](#0-3) [5](#0-4) 

Native ETH is represented by `address(0)` throughout the codebase (`Transferer.getERC20OrETHBalance`, `transferERC20TokenOrETH`): [6](#0-5) [7](#0-6) 

Nothing forces `address(0)` to be present in `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` whenever an `EmporiumOperation.value` moves ETH. In `runAction`, the operation loop runs unconditionally over `stack.ops`, actually spending Emporium's own ETH balance: [8](#0-7) 

If `stack.signerAddress == address(0)`, `verifyWallet` skips all signature checking (no EIP-712 signature is required at all) and just marks the message used: [9](#0-8) 

With `signerAddress == address(0)`, the "CASE 1: Stateful Interaction" branch (`op.invokeWallet && stack.signerAddress != address(0)`) is never taken, so every op falls into "CASE 2: Stateless Interaction", which the attacker fully controls (endpoint, callData, value), calling directly from the Emporium contract using Emporium's own ETH: [10](#0-9) 

**Attacker's exact call:** Go through the normal, legitimate `Hinkal.transact(a,b,c,dimensions,circomData)` path with `circomData.externalActionData.externalActionId` pointed at the already-registered Emporium action, and:
- `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses = [USDC]` (or any real token the attacker legitimately owns/spends a small, correctly-proved shielded amount of) — deliberately excluding `address(0)`.
- A valid ZK proof for a trivial/self-balanced USDC leg (e.g. deposit-and-withdraw an equal small amount, satisfying `dimensionsCheck`, `checkOnchainCreation`, nullifier/root checks, and the circuit's `inTotal + amountChanges === outTotal` constraint) so `verifyProof` passes normally — this is a real, valid, attacker-owned shielded operation and requires no bypass of proof/nullifier logic.
- `circomData.externalActionData.externalActionMetadata` = an ABI-encoded `EmporiumStack` with `signerAddress = address(0)` (skips signature check) and one `EmporiumOperation{ endpoint: attackerContract, invokeWallet: false, value: X, callData: anything-not-matching-the-two-forbidden-selectors }`, where `X` = `address(emporium).balance` (the entire ETH sitting in Emporium).

**Why guards fail:** `verifyProof`, `rootHashExists`, `performHinkalChecks`, `dimensionsCheck`, `checkOnchainCreation`, nullifier insertion, and the circuit's `inTotal+amountChanges===outTotal` constraint all validate only the USDC leg the attacker actually proved — none of them constrain, or even inspect, ETH. The `BalanceChangeShouldBePositive` check in `runAction` and the slippage/balance-equation check in `Hinkal.transact` both iterate strictly over `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses`, which simply doesn't contain `address(0)`, so the entire-balance ETH transfer to `attackerContract` inside the ops loop is invisible to every accounting check in the transaction.

### Impact Explanation
This is direct theft of funds held by the Emporium contract: any native ETH balance sitting at Emporium — whether from a prior legitimate `EmporiumOperation` (e.g., a partial/interrupted multi-step operation, unspent leftover from a swap/bridge call, or ETH intentionally routed through Emporium as an intermediate step) or from anyone mistakenly/deliberately sending ETH via `emporium.call{value:...}()` — can be swept in full to an attacker-controlled address. This qualifies as **Critical: direct theft of shielded or in-flight user funds** (or, depending on the ETH's provenance, at minimum **High: theft of protocol/relay fees or temporary/permanent freezing of user funds**), since the ETH may belong to other users' in-flight shielded transactions routed through Emporium. The attack is fully repeatable: every time ETH accumulates at Emporium (through any legitimate flow), the attacker can drain it again with a new transaction, paying only gas and a trivial relay/proof cost for an unrelated ERC20 leg.

### Likelihood Explanation
Preconditions are modest: Emporium must hold some nonzero ETH balance at the time of attack (accumulated from any earlier operation or stray transfer — plausible in normal usage since the Emporium pattern explicitly supports multi-step/stateful ops that can leave ETH behind, and the contract has no `receive`/fallback restriction preventing arbitrary ETH sends). The attacker needs no special privilege: they can deploy their own drain-target contract, generate a normal valid proof for their own owned UTXO(s) for an unrelated token, and freely craft `externalActionMetadata`, `EmporiumStack`, and `erc20TokenAddresses`, all explicitly listed as attacker-controlled inputs. No signature is required (`signerAddress = address(0)` path), no relayer, admin, or upgrade privilege is needed. This makes the attack cheap (gas + a minimal legitimate ERC20 round-trip) and reliably repeatable whenever the precondition balance exists.

### Recommendation
In `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction`, unconditionally include `address(0)` (native ETH) in the pre/post balance snapshot regardless of whether it appears in `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses`, and enforce the same `balanceChange < 0` guard on it. More generally, require that `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` (or a derived internal set) include every token actually moved by `op.value`/`op.callData` inside the ops loop — e.g., track total ETH sent across all `op.value` uses within `runAction` and require it not exceed ETH actually deposited during this call (`deltaAmountChanges` for the ETH slot), independent of whether the attacker chose to list `address(0)`. The same fix should be mirrored in `Hinkal.transact`'s balance-diff loop if it is also meant to protect Hinkal-level ETH.

### Proof of Concept
Foundry fork test plan:
1. Deploy `Hinkal`, `HinkalHelper`, `EmporiumUpgradeable` (registered as an external action via `registerExternalAction`), and a minimal `AttackerSink` contract with a `receive()`/fallback.
2. Seed Emporium with ETH to simulate stranded funds from a prior op: `vm.deal(address(emporium), 10 ether)` (representing "earlier `EmporiumOperation` with `op.value` funded by a legitimate flow" or a direct `emporium.call{value: 10 ether}("")`).
3. Attacker deposits/owns a small real UTXO of `USDC` (or any ERC20) sufficient to build a valid, self-balanced `deltaAmountChanges` leg, and generates a real Groth16 proof locally (snarkjs) for `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses = [USDC]` only.
4. Build `EmporiumStack{ signerAddress: address(0), ops: [EmporiumOperation{ endpoint: address(sink), invokeWallet:false, value: 10 ether, callData: "" }] }`, ABI-encode into `externalActionData.externalActionMetadata`.
5. Call `hinkal.transact(a,b,c,dimensions,circomData)` from the attacker EOA.
6. Assert: before the call, `address(sink).balance == 0` and `address(emporium).balance == 10 ether`; after the call, `address(sink).balance == 10 ether` and `address(emporium).balance == 0`, while `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` (USDC) balance-diff equality (`balanceDif == expected off/on-chain UTXO change`) holds exactly as before — proving ETH left the equality's scope entirely, i.e. `balancesAfter[ETH] - balancesBefore[ETH] != 0` was never checked against any authorized `deltaAmountChanges[ETH]`.
7. Confirm the transaction does not revert with `BalanceChangeShouldBePositive` or `"slippage param is violated"`, demonstrating the guard's blind spot.
