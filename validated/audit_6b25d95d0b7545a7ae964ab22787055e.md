### Title
Manipulable Aquarius reserve skew can suppress LP collateral prices and force profitable liquidations - ([File: common/src/oracle/lp.rs])

### Summary

Aquarius constant-product LP collateral is valued from the pool’s instantaneous reserves as `2 * sqrt(value_a * value_b) / total_shares`. A single attacker can skew those reserves through their own Aquarius trade, making the reported LP price arbitrarily low within the configured sanity band, liquidate an LP-collateralized account at the depressed valuation, and then reverse the trade. The stolen value is the excess collateral purchased with the liquidation bonus while the LP price is artificially suppressed.

### Finding Description

`aquarius::read` resolves the two underlying asset prices independently, reads the pool’s current reserves and total LP supply, then calls `fair_lp_price_wad`. [1](#0-0) 

For a constant-product pool, the resulting pool valuation is `2 * sqrt(value_a * value_b)`, rather than the sum of the current reserve values. [2](#0-1) 

If the pool begins with equal-value reserves and an attacker changes their value ratio to `r`, the reported value becomes `2 * sqrt(r) / (1 + r)` of the balanced value. For example, a 16:1 reserve-value skew reports approximately `8 / 17 = 47%` of the balanced LP price.

The observation timestamp comes only from the two external leg prices, so a fresh reserve manipulation does not make the LP observation stale. [3](#0-2) 

The controller uses this synchronously fetched price to compute collateral value, weighted collateral, debt, and health factor. [4](#0-3) 

The sanity check only rejects prices outside the configured minimum and maximum; it does not compare the LP result against a historical observation or detect reserve-ratio manipulation. [5](#0-4) 

### Impact Explanation

An attacker can temporarily push a victim’s LP collateral below its manipulation-resistant fair value, causing the victim’s health factor to fall below 1 even though the LP redemption economics have not deteriorated by the same amount.

At the depressed LP price, the liquidation calculation denominates seized collateral in more LP units per unit of repaid debt. After receiving the discounted LP collateral and liquidation bonus, the attacker reverses the Aquarius trade and can redeem or sell the LP collateral near its recovered value.

This permits theft of user collateral through forced liquidation. It does not require compromising an external price provider: the manipulated input is the Aquarius pool’s own current reserve state, which the oracle deliberately reads inside the same call.

### Likelihood Explanation

The attack requires a listed Aquarius LP collateral market, a victim close enough to liquidation that the achievable reserve skew crosses HF 1, and enough capital to move the configured pool.

Large relative skews are needed for large price suppression, but the attacker can be the liquidity provider whose collateral is being manipulated and can use an attacker contract to perform the Aquarius trade, controller liquidation, and reversal in one transaction. The expected profitability is bounded by the victim’s liquidation size and bonus versus swap fees and residual slippage.

This is a single-address, unprivileged path: attacker contract executes an allowed Aquarius swap, calls the controller’s public `liquidate` entrypoint against the victim’s `account_id` and outstanding debt asset, receives the collateral payout, and then executes the reverse Aquarius swap. No governance, private-key, upgrade, or privileged oracle access is required.

### Recommendation

Do not derive liquidation-sensitive LP collateral prices solely from instantaneous Aquarius reserves.

Use a time-weighted or otherwise history-backed LP price bound for risk operations, such as:

- storing accepted LP prices and rejecting liquidation use when the instantaneous result deviates beyond a narrow movement bound;
- requiring an independent LP valuation source with a strict two-leg tolerance;
- deriving LP collateral value from manipulation-resistant underlying redemption accounting plus a bounded historical reserve ratio;
- adding a per-invocation reserve-ratio sanity bound rather than only a final USD sanity band.

Any mitigation should still reject direct reserve donations or share-supply anomalies and preserve the existing minimum-pool-value check. [6](#0-5) 

### Proof of Concept

Assume an Aquarius LP collateral market whose pool initially contains reserves with equal external value `V` in each leg.

1. The attacker contract swaps enough of asset A into the Aquarius pool to produce a 16:1 reserve-value ratio, leaving approximately `16V` of A value and `V` of B value after accounting for the pool’s invariant.

2. The controller liquidation call loads the victim’s LP asset through `Context::load_markets`, which calls the configured price aggregator and caches the resulting feed for the invocation. [7](#0-6) [8](#0-7) 

3. `aquarius::read` obtains the manipulated reserves and passes both leg values to `fair_lp_price_wad`. [9](#0-8) 

4. The reported LP pool value becomes `2 * sqrt(16V * V) = 8V`, while the balanced reference value is `32V`, so the LP share price is approximately 25% of the balanced reserve-value convention and approximately 47% of the normal `2 * sqrt(V * V)` fair-price convention.

5. If the victim’s LP collateral and debt initially produce HF slightly above 1, the suppressed collateral valuation makes `weighted_collateral / total_debt` fall below 1. [10](#0-9) 

6. The attacker calls the public `liquidate` entrypoint for the victim `account_id`, supplies repayment for the victim’s outstanding debt asset, and receives LP collateral priced using the manipulated feed. Because collateral quantity is denominated by the depressed price, the attacker receives materially more LP shares than at the unmanipulated price, in addition to the liquidation bonus.

7. The attacker reverses the Aquarius swap. The LP collateral retains its claim on the restored pool reserves, while the victim has already lost the excess LP shares seized during the artificially low valuation.

8. The attacker’s cost is swap fees plus residual round-trip slippage; profit is the discounted collateral plus liquidation bonus minus those costs. The result is theft of user collateral rather than a self-limiting protocol loss.
