This confirms the analog. The gem's documentation explicitly tells developers `data.shop` is "the shop domain of the webhook" — a trusted, verified tenant identifier — and shows it being used directly for tenant-scoped work (`perform_later(topic: data.topic, shop_domain: data.shop, ...)`) [1](#0-0) . The gem's own `Registry.process` performs no additional check binding `request.shop` to the HMAC-verified bytes; it directly forwards `request.shop` into `WebhookMetadata` after only validating the HMAC over the body [2](#0-1) .

### Title
Webhook `shop` domain is not bound to the HMAC signature, allowing shop identity spoofing via valid replayed payloads - (File: lib/shopify_api/webhooks/request.rb)

### Summary
`ShopifyAPI::Webhooks::Registry.process` authenticates an incoming webhook solely by validating an HMAC over the raw request body, then trusts the `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain` header verbatim as the tenant identity passed to the app's handler. Because the header is not part of the HMAC-signed material, an attacker who possesses any one genuine `(raw_body, hmac)` pair signed with the app's shared `api_secret_key` can replay it with a forged shop-domain header, causing the app to process the payload as if it came from an arbitrary victim shop.

### Finding Description
`ShopifyAPI::Utils::HmacValidator.validate` computes `OpenSSL::HMAC.hexdigest` over `verifiable_query.to_signable_string` and compares it to the received HMAC [3](#0-2) . For webhooks, `to_signable_string` is defined as simply `@raw_body` — it does not incorporate any header, including the shop domain [4](#0-3) . Meanwhile, `shop` is read directly from the `shopify-shop-domain` / `x-shopify-shop-domain` header, entirely independent of the signed body [5](#0-4) .

`Registry.process` validates only the HMAC and then forwards `request.shop` unchanged into `WebhookMetadata`, which is delivered to the app's handler as the authoritative tenant identity: `handler.handle(data: WebhookMetadata.new(topic: request.topic, shop: request.shop, body: request.parsed_body, ...))` [2](#0-1) . There is no check anywhere in the gem that the `shop-domain` header actually corresponds to the shop that the webhook subscription was registered for, nor is the shop bound cryptographically to the payload.

The binding that is broken: `shop_verified_by_HMAC == shop_delivered_to_handler` does not hold. The HMAC verifies `{raw_body}` signed under `api_secret_key`; the `shop` field consumed by application code is taken from an entirely separate, unauthenticated header.

Because `api_secret_key` (the app's `client_secret`) is shared across every shop that installs the app — it is not per-tenant — any merchant who installs the app (an unprivileged, unauthenticated party from the perspective of any other tenant) can trigger webhooks for their own shop, capture the genuine `(raw_body, X-Shopify-Hmac-Sha256)` pair Shopify sends them, and then replay that exact body/HMAC pair to the app's webhook endpoint while substituting a different, victim shop's domain in the `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain` header. `HmacValidator.validate` will still pass (it never looks at the header), and the handler will receive `data.shop == victim-shop.myshopify.com` despite the payload never having originated from, nor being authorized for, that shop.

### Impact Explanation
This is a cross-tenant identity confusion: an attacker who legitimately controls one shop (installer of the app) can make the app process arbitrary, attacker-chosen webhook bodies "as" a different shop of their choosing. Depending on how the host app uses `data.shop` (as shown in the gem's own documented example, to key job/queue processing, look up sessions, or fetch shop-specific configuration/access tokens for further actions) [6](#0-5) , this can lead to cross-tenant data corruption, spoofed events attributed to a victim's shop, or triggering shop-scoped side effects (e.g., using the victim shop's stored access token to act on Shopify's API in response to attacker-supplied webhook content) — satisfying the "cross-tenant access" impact bucket.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is non-trivial but requires the attacker to (a) be an installer of the target app themselves (which is normal, unprivileged access any merchant can obtain) and (b) know or guess a target shop's `.myshopify.com` domain (public information) to substitute into the header. No possession of `api_secret_key`, access tokens, or the victim's credentials is required — only a genuine payload/HMAC from the attacker's own installation, which they can trivially trigger themselves (e.g. `app/uninstalled`, `shop/update`, or any topic whose payload content is attacker-influenced or irrelevant to the exploit).

### Recommendation
Bind the shop domain into the material verified by the HMAC (or, at minimum, cross-check `request.shop` against the shop recorded when the specific `webhook_id` subscription was registered) before constructing `WebhookMetadata`. The gem should not present `data.shop` as a trusted value on the sole basis of the body HMAC passing; either include the shop-domain header in `to_signable_string`, or validate that the delivered webhook's `shop-domain`/`webhook_id` pairing matches a known registration for that shop before invoking the handler.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker installs the target app onto their own shop `attacker.myshopify.com` and registers/triggers a webhook (e.g. `app/uninstalled`) which the app is subscribed to.
2. Shopify delivers a legitimate request to the app's webhook endpoint with headers `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain: attacker.myshopify.com`, `X-Shopify-Hmac-Sha256: <valid-hmac-of-body>`, and some `raw_body`.
3. Attacker captures `raw_body` and `X-Shopify-Hmac-Sha256` from this legitimate delivery (e.g., via their own logging endpoint, or intercepting their own traffic).
4. Attacker sends a new POST to the same app webhook endpoint, keeping `raw_body` and `X-Shopify-Hmac-Sha256` identical, but replacing `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain` with `victim-shop.myshopify.com`.
5. `ShopifyAPI::Utils::HmacValidator.validate` recomputes the HMAC over `raw_body` only [7](#0-6)  — validation succeeds because the body is unchanged.
6. `Registry.process` calls the handler with `WebhookMetadata(shop: "victim-shop.myshopify.com", ...)` [8](#0-7) , and the app processes the attacker's payload under the victim shop's identity.
