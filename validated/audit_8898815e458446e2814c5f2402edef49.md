### Title
Unauthorized theft of assets held by shared `EmporiumUpgradeable` vault via unauthenticated CASE 2 raw calls - (File: `contracts/external-actions/emporium/upgradeable/EmporiumUpgradeable.sol`)

### Summary
`EmporiumUpgradeable` is a single shared contract that acts as the `msg.sender`/owner of assets between and during user operations. Its balance-conservation check in `runAction` only tracks tokens listed in the caller-supplied `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` array, and CASE 2 ops execute an arbitrary `op.endpoint.call(op.callData)` with Emporium itself as `msg.sender`, with no check tying the operation's target/effect to the account that authorized the underlying UTXOs. Any asset (e.g. an ERC721 LP position) that ends up owned by Emporium but is not one of the tracked ERC20 addresses is completely invisible to the accounting equality and can be swept out by any subsequent, unrelated caller.

### Finding Description
The equality that is supposed to hold is: *AUTHORITY* — every state change effected by an `op` executed inside `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction` must have been authorized by the account whose funds/UTXOs are being spent (via `verifyWallet`'s EIP-712 signature check when `stack.signerAddress != address(0)`, or implicitly via the balance-in/balance-out equality for tokens tracked in `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses`).

In practice, neither guard holds for CASE 2 ("Stateless Interaction") operations:

- `verifyWallet` in [1](#0-0)  only recovers and checks a signature `if (stack.signerAddress != address(0))`; a caller can simply set `signerAddress = address(0)` and skip authentication entirely for their own `EmporiumStack`.
- CASE 2 in `runAction` performs a completely unrestricted low-level call: `(success, err) = op.endpoint.call{value: op.value}(op.callData);` [2](#0-1)  — the only restriction is blocking the `callHinkalWallet`/`doSendToRelay` selectors; there is no allowlist of `op.endpoint` targets and no check that the call's effect stays within the caller's own funds.
- `onlyAllowedRecipient` only checks that `msg.sender` (i.e. the `Hinkal` contract) is a registered recipient [3](#0-2) ; it says nothing about which end-user is driving the call.
- The balance-conservation loop in `runAction` computes `balancesBefore`/`balancesAfter` and enforces `balanceChange >= 0` and downstream distribution to `msg.sender` (the attacker's own transact call) purely over `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses`, an array chosen by the caller of that specific transaction [4](#0-3) . `getBalancesForArray`/`getERC20OrETHBalance` only understand ERC20/ETH `balanceOf`, never ERC721 `ownerOf` [5](#0-4) .
- `Transferer.onERC721Received` unconditionally accepts any NFT sent to Emporium [6](#0-5) .

Exploit flow:
1. Victim calls `Hinkal.transact` with `externalActionData.externalAddress = EmporiumUpgradeable` and a CASE 2 op whose `endpoint` is an attacker-deployed Uniswap-V3-style pool; the op's `callData` calls `mint()`/`deposit()`, causing the pool to `safeTransferFrom` the resulting LP NFT to `msg.sender` (Emporium), which `onERC721Received` happily accepts. Because the LP NFT's contract address is not one of the victim's `erc20TokenAddresses`, it is never observed by `balancesBefore`/`balancesAfter`, so the victim's transaction completes normally.
2. Attacker then submits their own, unrelated `Hinkal.transact` call, again with `externalAddress = EmporiumUpgradeable`, `stack.signerAddress = address(0)` (skipping `verifyWallet`'s signature check), and a CASE 2 op whose `endpoint` is the LP NFT contract and `callData` is `transferFrom(EmporiumUpgradeable, attacker, tokenId)`. Since Emporium is the actual on-chain owner of the token and the low-level call makes Emporium the `msg.sender`/caller as seen by the NFT contract, standard ERC721 authorization (`_isApprovedOrOwner(msg.sender, tokenId)`) passes, and the NFT transfers to the attacker.
3. The attacker's own `erc20TokenAddresses` list need not include the NFT contract at all, so none of the balance/slippage equalities in `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction` (lines 132-151) or in `Hinkal.transact` (balance-diff checks) are ever violated — the theft is invisible to every accounting check in the protocol.

Root cause: `EmporiumUpgradeable` is architected as a shared, stateful vault whose only safety mechanism is a per-transaction ERC20/ETH balance-delta reconciliation; it has no mechanism to bind a CASE 2 raw call's target/effects to the authorizing party, and no way to represent/track non-ERC20 assets (or ERC20 assets simply omitted from the token list) that happen to be resident in the contract between transactions.

### Impact Explanation
This is a direct, permissionless theft of assets belonging to another party out of a shared, non-custodial-by-design vault contract — matching the "Critical: direct theft of shielded or in-flight user funds" category. Any account owned by Emporium (LP NFTs, ERC1155s, or even ERC20 tokens simply left off a victim's own `erc20TokenAddresses` list) can be drained by any subsequent unrelated caller with zero prior deposit relationship to the victim. The attack is repeatable against every victim who leaves any asset resident in Emporium across two separate operations (e.g. any multi-step DeFi position build-up such as mint-then-stake, or NFT-yielding LPs).

### Likelihood Explanation
The precondition (some asset resident in Emporium that is outside the caller-declared `erc20TokenAddresses` set, e.g. an LP position NFT) is easily reachable by any user who performs a multi-step interaction (e.g., minting a Uniswap V3 position) through the CASE 2 path, and the attacker requires no special privilege — just the ability to submit their own `Hinkal.transact` with a self-crafted `EmporiumStack` (`signerAddress = 0`, no signature needed) and a CASE 2 op targeting the known NFT contract/tokenId. Attacker cost is one transaction and gas; the exploit is fully repeatable for any future occurrence of the same pattern.

### Recommendation
Do not allow CASE 2 raw calls to have unrestricted, un-attributable side effects on Emporium's holdings. Options: (1) require every CASE 2 op's `endpoint` to be on a governance-controlled allowlist and forbid calls whose target is a token/NFT contract already known to hold Emporium-custodied value unless authorized by that value's original depositor; (2) require `stack.signerAddress` (and its EIP-712 signature) to be mandatory for any operation, closing the `signerAddress == address(0)` bypass in `verifyWallet`; (3) extend the balance-conservation mechanism to track and account for non-ERC20 assets (NFTs) acquired/lost during `runAction`, reverting if an untracked asset balance changes; (4) never let Emporium retain custody of assets across transactions — force atomic "receive-then-forward-to-owner-in-same-tx" semantics for any output of a CASE 2 call.

### Proof of Concept
Foundry fork test plan:
1. Deploy a mock Uniswap-V3-style pool that mints an ERC721 LP position and `safeTransferFrom`s it to `msg.sender` on `mint()`/`deposit()`.
2. Deploy `EmporiumUpgradeable` behind its proxy, register it as an allowed recipient on `Hinkal`.
3. As the "victim," call `Hinkal.transact` with `externalActionData.externalAddress = Emporium`, `EmporiumStack.ops = [ { invokeWallet: false, endpoint: mockPool, callData: mint(...) } ]`, and `circomData.erc20TokenAddresses` excluding the LP NFT contract. Assert the mock pool's NFT `ownerOf(tokenId) == address(Emporium)` and that `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction`'s `balancesBefore == balancesAfter` equality over `erc20TokenAddresses` never reverted.
4. As the "attacker" (different EOA, no relationship to the victim's UTXOs), call `Hinkal.transact` again with `externalAddress = Emporium`, `EmporiumStack.signerAddress = address(0)`, `ops = [ { invokeWallet: false, endpoint: address(mockPool_NFT), callData: transferFrom(Emporium, attacker, tokenId) } ]`.
5. Assert the call succeeds (`success == true` inside `runAction`), and `ownerOf(tokenId) == attacker` afterward, while none of `Hinkal.transact`'s balance-diff `require`s (lines 111-146 of `contracts/Hinkal.sol`) or `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction`'s balance equality reverted, proving the theft was completely unaccounted for by both equalities.
