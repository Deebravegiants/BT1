### Title
Caller-controlled swap routes can hide wallet-draining contract calls - ([File: contracts/controller/src/strategies/swap.rs](contracts/controller/src/strategies/swap.rs))

### Summary
High-severity authorization-scope issue: `swap_collateral`, `swap_debt`, `repay_debt_with_collateral`, and `multiply` accept caller-supplied opaque route bytes and execute them through the configured router, allowing a malicious route venue to add an unrelated token transfer beneath the caller’s signed authorization tree. [1](#0-0) [2](#0-1) 

### Finding Description
`StrategySwap` is only an opaque `Bytes` payload, so the controller does not structurally validate or constrain the external contracts named by the route. [2](#0-1)  For example, `swap_collateral` authorizes the account owner or delegate, withdraws collateral to controller custody, and forwards the caller’s route unchanged into the swap boundary. [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) 

`swap_tokens` invokes `execute_strategy(sender = controller, total_in, swap_xdr)` and measures only the controller’s `token_in` spend and `token_out` receipt. [5](#0-4) [6](#0-5)  Those checks do not enumerate the route’s nested calls and therefore cannot detect an unrelated `transfer(victim, attacker, amount)` executed by a malicious venue during the swap. [6](#0-5) 

The controller’s invoker authorization is narrow for its own funds—it authorizes only `token_in.transfer(controller, router, amount_in)`—but this does not bound the caller’s separate transaction authorization for calls made deeper by route-selected contracts. [7](#0-6)  Consequently, a crafted route can produce the expected collateral output while its malicious venue adds a wallet-token transfer to the simulated authorization tree; if the victim signs that tree, the unrelated transfer executes atomically with an otherwise successful strategy. [5](#0-4) [8](#0-7) 

### Impact Explanation
A victim can lose unrelated wallet tokens that were never supplied to the lending protocol and were not declared as swap input. [5](#0-4)  Because the malicious route can still return a positive measured output, the controller’s overspend, zero-output, and post-strategy solvency checks do not prevent the theft. [6](#0-5) [9](#0-8) 

### Likelihood Explanation
The attack requires the victim to sign a transaction containing the malicious route and its expanded authorization tree, but the route is opaque bytes and an honest-looking quote can conceal the extra child invocation from users who do not decode the full authorization tree. [2](#0-1) [10](#0-9)  All strategy entrypoints that take route bytes share this exposure whenever they perform a distinct-asset swap. [1](#0-0) 

### Recommendation
Constrain routes to an allowlist of venue pool addresses, or have the router cryptographically commit to and enforce a bounded list of expected external invocations for each route. Reject any route whose execution can introduce a caller-authorized invocation other than the declared input pull, and require clients to display and verify the complete authorization tree before signing. [10](#0-9) [11](#0-10) 

### Proof of Concept
1. The victim owns a Normal account with supplied USDC collateral and separately holds `VICTIM_TOKEN` in their wallet. [12](#0-11) 
2. The attacker deploys a malicious contract implementing the venue pool ABI expected by the route and prefunds it with enough `ETH` to produce the quoted output. [8](#0-7) 
3. The attacker constructs `route_xdr` for a USDC→ETH swap whose venue address is that malicious pool. [13](#0-12) 
4. The victim is induced to submit `swap_collateral(caller=victim, account_id, current=USDC_market, amount, new=ETH_market, swap=route_xdr)`. [13](#0-12) 
5. The controller withdraws the victim’s USDC collateral and calls the configured router with the attacker’s opaque route. [14](#0-13) [15](#0-14) 
6. During the route’s venue call, the malicious pool performs `VICTIM_TOKEN.transfer(victim, attacker, victim_balance)` while also delivering enough ETH output for the swap to satisfy the measured-output check. [6](#0-5) [8](#0-7) 
7. Transaction simulation places the unexpected wallet-token transfer beneath the victim’s `swap_collateral` authorization; when the victim signs the poisoned tree, the token contract accepts the nested transfer and the account still receives valid swapped collateral. [3](#0-2) [16](#0-15)

```rust
/// Authorizes, on behalf of the current contract, one `transfer(from, to, amount)`
/// call on `token_addr` made deeper in the next contract call (for example by
/// the pool). The entry allows no further sub-invocations.
pub fn authorize_transfer_as_current(
    env: &Env,
    token_addr: &Address,
    from: &Address,
    to: &Address,
    amount: i128,
) {
    let entry = InvokerContractAuthEntry::Contract(SubContractInvocation {
        context: ContractContext {
            contract: token_addr.clone(),
            fn_name: symbol_short!("transfer"),
            args: (from.clone(), to.clone(), amount).into_val(env),
        },
        sub_invocations: Vec::new(env),
    });
    env.authorize_as_current_contract(vec![env, entry]);
```
