The self-liquidation griefing path is not fully closed because the only defense (`ERR-LIQUIDATION-BORROW-SAME-BLOCK`) only blocks the atomic single-block variant, not the "borrow now, wait for a legitimate price move, self-liquidate later" variant.

### Title
Self-liquidation across separate blocks bypasses same-block protection and lets a borrower extract collateral while socializing bad debt to lenders - (File: `mainnet/contracts/market/v0-4-market.clar`)

### Summary
`liquidate()` in `v0-4-market.clar` only blocks same-block borrow-then-liquidate sequences via `last-borrow-block`, but does nothing to prevent a borrower from opening a maximum-LTV position and later, once a legitimate oracle price update pushes their own position past `LTV-LIQ-FULL`, self-liquidating to sweep all remaining collateral while writing off the unpaid debt as socialized bad debt.

### Finding Description
In `liquidate()`, health/threshold checks use per-egroup `LTV-BORROW`, `LTV-LIQ-PARTIAL`, `LTV-LIQ-FULL`, and a graduated penalty between `LIQ-PENALTY-MIN`/`LIQ-PENALTY-MAX` [1](#0-0) . The only defense against a borrower liquidating their own just-opened position is the `last-borrow-block` check, which reverts only if the current block equals the block in which the position last borrowed [2](#0-1) .

This check does not prevent a two-step attack spanning separate blocks: the attacker (1) borrows at close to `LTV-BORROW` in block N, then (2) after a subsequent, legitimate oracle price update (pushed by anyone via the in-band `price-feeds` parameter or by Pyth's own keeper) drives their own `current-ltv` past `LTV-LIQ-FULL` in a later block, (3) calls `liquidate()` on their own position as `liquidator`. Since `last-borrow-block` (block N) no longer equals the current block, the check passes.

Once the position's LTV is deep enough that the graduated liquidation math cannot fully cover the debt from the "expected" collateral share, the remaining collateral is swept entirely to the liquidator and the unpaid remainder is written off via `socialize-debt-asset` → `vault-socialize-debt`, spreading the loss across vault depositors [3](#0-2) [4](#0-3) [5](#0-4) . The self-liquidator (as both borrower and liquidator) thus receives `user-coll-balance` in collateral while only being charged with `debt-final`/`debt-to-repay`, and the shortfall is socialized rather than borne by the attacker — breaking the identity `collateral_seized_usd == debt_repaid_usd * (1 + liq_penalty)` in the attacker's favor.

This is the same underlying incentive flaw described in the Morpho report (fixed liquidation-incentive math plus a single oracle tick creating bad debt that the liquidator can capture), but Zest's `LTV-BORROW`/`LTV-LIQ-FULL` gap (e.g. 70%/85%/95%, or as low as 20%/30%/40% for riskier egroups, per `mainnet/contracts/proposals/mainnet/v0-init.clar` egroup definitions) requires a much larger single price move than Morpho's design (where LLTV served as both borrow limit and liquidation threshold) [6](#0-5) .

### Impact Explanation
If exploited, this results in permanent loss of principal for lenders in the affected vault, since the socialized bad debt directly reduces the value backing outstanding `zToken` shares — equivalent to protocol insolvency for that debt asset's vault to the extent of the write-off. This matches the Critical impact bar (theft of principal funds / protocol insolvency).

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is low-to-medium and considerably lower than in Morpho's design, because Zest requires the collateral price to fall enough to push the position from `LTV-BORROW` all the way past `LTV-LIQ-FULL` (a substantially larger single-update move, e.g., >25% for the sBTC/USDC egroup, or more for conservative egroups) rather than just a few percent past a shared LLTV. It also requires the attacker to be patient (borrow now, liquidate on any later block after a qualifying price update) rather than executing atomically, which the code's same-block check does successfully block. The attack is realistic primarily for markets pairing genuinely volatile collateral with permissive egroup LTV settings, or during flash-crash events.

### Recommendation
- Extend the same-block/anti-sandwich protection to a cooldown window (e.g., N blocks) between a position's last borrow/collateral-increase action and eligibility for that same account to act as `liquidator` on their own position, rather than only comparing against the exact current block.
- Consider disallowing `borrower == liquidator` (or `tx-sender == borrower`) entirely, or capping the collateral a self-liquidator can withdraw to no more than the "expected" (non-swept) amount, pushing any true bad debt shortfall through the normal socialization path without granting the self-liquidator the full remaining collateral windfall.
- Track and alert on egroups where realized collateral volatility has drifted such that `LTV-BORROW`/`LTV-LIQ-FULL` no longer provide an adequate buffer against a single oracle update, and support lowering `LTV-BORROW` per egroup as a live risk-management lever.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker deposits collateral and borrows debt asset up to `LTV-BORROW` for an egroup (e.g., sBTC/USDC egroup: `LTV-BORROW=70%`, `LTV-LIQ-FULL=95%`) [7](#0-6) , in block N. `last-borrow-block` is recorded as N.
2. In a subsequent block (N+k, k≥1), collateral price legitimately drops enough that `current-ltv` computed in `liquidate()` exceeds `LTV-LIQ-FULL` [8](#0-7) .
3. Attacker calls `liquidate()` on their own position as `liquidator`. The `same-block-check` passes because `last-borrow-block (N) != stacks-block-height (N+k)` [2](#0-1) .
4. Because the position is deep in bad debt territory, `remaining-debt-to-repay` resolves to 0 and `coll-final` is set to the full `user-coll-balance`, i.e. the attacker/liquidator receives all remaining collateral [9](#0-8) .
5. The unpaid debt remainder is written off via `socialize-debt-asset`/`vault-socialize-debt`, socializing the loss to lenders in the debt asset's vault [4](#0-3) .
6. Net effect: attacker recovers all deposited collateral (or more in USD terms than the debt they actually repay) while lenders absorb the shortfall.
