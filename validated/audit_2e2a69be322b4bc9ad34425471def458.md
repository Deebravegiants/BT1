The HMAC in `ShopifyAPI::Webhooks::Request` only signs `@raw_body` [1](#0-0) , while `topic`, `shop`, `webhook_id`, and `api_version` are read directly from unauthenticated HTTP headers [2](#0-1) . `HmacValidator.validate` only proves that `body` was signed with the app's shared `api_secret_key` — it says nothing about which shop or topic the header claims belong to [3](#0-2) . `Registry.process` then trusts `request.shop` as the tenant identity and forwards it straight into `WebhookMetadata` handed to the app's handler [4](#0-3) [5](#0-4) .

### Title
Webhook shop/topic identity headers are not covered by the HMAC signature, allowing cross-tenant webhook spoofing - (File: lib/shopify_api/webhooks/request.rb)

### Summary
`ShopifyAPI::Webhooks::Request#to_signable_string` returns only the raw request body, and `HmacValidator.validate` verifies the HMAC solely over that body using the app's single, shared `api_secret_key`. The `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain`, `X-Shopify-Topic`, and `X-Shopify-Webhook-Id` headers are never part of the signed payload, so the gem provides no cryptographic binding between "this HMAC-valid body" and "this claimed shop." Because `api_secret_key` is identical for every shop that installs the app (it's not per-shop), any user who controls one shop installation of the app can legitimately trigger a real webhook, capture its exact `(raw_body, hmac)` pair, and replay that pair to the app's public webhook endpoint with the `shop-domain` header swapped for a victim shop. `ShopifyAPI::Utils::HmacValidator.validate` will accept it, and `Registry.process` will hand the app's handler a `WebhookMetadata` claiming the body belongs to the victim shop [4](#0-3) .

### Finding Description
The identity binding that should hold is: `shop` claimed in the processed webhook == `shop` that actually produced the HMAC-signed body. Before the request: an attacker-controlled shop A triggers/receives a legitimate Shopify webhook, giving them a valid `(raw_body, hmac-sha256 header)` pair signed with the app's `api_secret_key` (shared across all shops for a given app, per Shopify's design) [6](#0-5) . After the attacker's request: they POST that identical `raw_body` and `hmac-sha256` header to the app's webhook endpoint, but substitute `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain` (and optionally `X-Shopify-Topic`/`X-Shopify-Webhook-Id`) with a victim shop's values. `Request#hmac` and `Request#to_signable_string` never look at these headers [7](#0-6) , so `HmacValidator.validate(request)` still returns `true` [8](#0-7) . `Registry.process` proceeds to dispatch `WebhookMetadata.new(topic: request.topic, shop: request.shop, ...)` to the app's handler, which is the sole tenant-identifying data the gem exposes to the host application [9](#0-8) .

### Impact Explanation
This breaks the tenant boundary the gem is supposed to enforce for webhook processing: any low-privilege actor who can install the app on any shop (including a free/dev store they control) can forge webhook events that the app will attribute to an arbitrary victim shop, since the "authenticated" identity (HMAC-proven body) and the "acted-upon" identity (shop header) are decoupled. Depending on the handler's use of `data.shop` (e.g., resolving which merchant's session/token/data to update), this can lead to cross-tenant data corruption or injection under another merchant's identity, which falls under the Critical "cross-tenant access" category.

### Likelihood Explanation
Exploitation requires only that the attacker control one shop installation of the target app — a low bar, since attackers can install most public/dev apps on their own store for free — and be able to trigger any subscribed webhook topic for that shop (e.g., placing a test order, updating a product). No access to the app's `client_secret`, no TLS interception, and no privileged account is required; capturing one's own legitimate webhook delivery and replaying it with a modified header is straightforward.

### Recommendation
Bind the shop (and ideally topic/webhook id) into the signed payload verification, e.g., verify HMAC over `shop-domain + raw_body` (or otherwise cryptographically tie the claimed shop header to the signature), or cross-check `request.shop` against a shop the app has an active, previously-issued offline session/token for before dispatching to the handler, rejecting webhooks for shops the app has no record of installing.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker installs the target app on shop `attacker.myshopify.com` and completes OAuth (permitted, unprivileged action).
2. Attacker triggers a subscribed webhook topic (e.g. `products/update`) on their own shop, capturing the raw POST body and the `X-Shopify-Hmac-Sha256` header sent by Shopify to the app's webhook endpoint.
3. Attacker replays that exact body and HMAC header to the app's public webhook endpoint, replacing `X-Shopify-Shop-Domain` with `victim-shop.myshopify.com`.
4. `ShopifyAPI::Webhooks::Request#hmac`/`#to_signable_string` ignore the shop header [7](#0-6) ; `HmacValidator.validate` succeeds because it only checks the body [3](#0-2) .
5. `Registry.process` invokes the app's handler with `WebhookMetadata(shop: "victim-shop.myshopify.com", ...)`, causing the app to act on the attacker's payload as if it came from the victim shop [4](#0-3) .
