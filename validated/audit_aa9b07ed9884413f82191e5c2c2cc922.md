### Title
Supply-index floor resurrects wiped-out supplier claims after full bad-debt write-down, letting stale shares drain fresh deposits - (File: contracts/pool/src/interest.rs)

### Summary
`apply_bad_debt_to_supply_index` socializes bad debt by scaling the market's `supply_index` down proportionally to the loss, but clamps the result upward at `SUPPLY_INDEX_FLOOR_RAW` (`RAY/1000`) instead of allowing it to reach zero. When socialized bad debt equals or exceeds the total supplied value, every supplier's claim should be written to zero; the floor instead leaves all unburned supply shares with a positive residual value. These "stranded" shares are a claim on *nothing* — they survive the logical destruction of the market's backing and can later be redeemed against cash deposited by new suppliers. This is the direct analog of a use-after-free: value (shares) remains usable after the backing it pointed to was released (socialized to zero).

### Finding Description
`contracts/pool/src/interest.rs:73-89`:

```rust
let capped = bad_debt.min(total_supplied_value);
let remaining = total_supplied_value.checked_sub(cache.env(), capped);
let reduction_factor = remaining.div_floor(cache.env(), total_supplied_value);
let new_supply_index = cache.supply_index().mul_floor(cache.env(), reduction_factor);
cache.set_supply_index(new_supply_index.max(Ray::from(SUPPLY_INDEX_FLOOR_RAW)));
```

When `bad_debt >= total_supplied_value`, `remaining` is zero, so `new_supply_index` is zero — but `.max(Ray::from(SUPPLY_INDEX_FLOOR_RAW))` raises it to `RAY/1000`. Every `supplied` share is then worth `shares × RAY/1000 > 0` while the market holds no corresponding backing.

The pool's own unit test proves the exploit mechanics end to end: `contracts/pool/tests/interest.rs:430-495` (`test_raw_cache_seizure_residual_would_drain_fresh_cash_without_supply_guard`) drives a cache through a full wipeout, shows a survivor keeps a positive `unscale_supply_floor` claim, has a new depositor `credit_cash`, and shows `resolve_withdrawal(i128::MAX, alice_scaled)` paying the stale shareholder *exactly* the fresh deposit, leaving the honest depositor under-collateralized.

Reachability from the unprivileged surface: `controller::clean_bad_debt` (`contracts/controller/src/lib.rs:163`) → `socialize_bad_debt`/`execute_bad_debt_cleanup` (`contracts/controller/src/positions/liquidation/bad_debt.rs:14-60`) → `pool_seize_positions_call` → `apply_bad_debt_to_supply_index`. The gate only requires `total_debt > total_collateral` and collateral ≤ $5 dust (`is_socializable_bad_debt`, `apply.rs:313`). `docs/reference/invariants.md` INV-LIQ-04 confirms "no final account-health or full-backing assertion" runs after cleanup and that "the index floor can leave a shortfall" — recapitalize can inject cash, but it does not restore the index or delete the stranded claims, so injected recap funds are themselves payable to stale shares first.

### Impact Explanation
Theft of user funds / permanent freezing. After a full write-down, any holder of legacy supply shares retains a claim worth ~0.1% of nominal share count that has zero backing. The first fresh `supply` (or `recapitalize`) into that market creates real cash the stranded shares can immediately withdraw via `withdraw`, transferring the new depositor's funds to the stale shareholder and leaving the honest depositor with an unbacked position. The stale shareholder need not be the attacker who triggered cleanup — they can simply be a pre-existing supplier who never withdrew.

### Likelihood Explanation
Reaching `bad_debt >= total_supplied_value` requires a market where unpaid debt grows to exceed all supply — possible when a large borrower becomes deeply insolvent after interest accrual and the market's other suppliers have largely exited, or in thin markets where a single insolvent account dominates. Cleanup itself is permissionless (`clean_bad_debt`, dust-gated; `force_socialize_bad_debt` owner-only for larger collateral). The attacker position is simply "be a supplier in a market that fully wipes out," which requires no privilege. Likelihood is moderate-to-low because it needs a near-total wipeout, but the payoff — draining the next depositor in full — is direct theft, qualifying as High severity per the impact criteria (theft of user funds).

### Recommendation
When `bad_debt >= total_supplied_value` (i.e., `remaining == 0`), zero the market correctly: burn or freeze all outstanding supply shares rather than clamping the index to a nonzero floor, and/or block new `supply` mints into a market whose index sits at `SUPPLY_INDEX_FLOOR_RAW` until `recapitalize` restores backing proportional to outstanding claims. At minimum, gate withdrawals on `require_backed_market` (or a per-share backing check) so stranded claims cannot be paid out of fresh cash.

### Proof of Concept
1. Market M has supplier S holding shares and borrower account B; accrual/price movement makes B's debt exceed M's total supplied value (or B's debt ≥ supply after S is the dominant survivor).
2. B is liquidated down to dust collateral; anyone calls `clean_bad_debt(caller, B_account_id)`. `execute_bad_debt_cleanup` seizes B's positions and calls `apply_bad_debt_to_supply_index` with `bad_debt ≥ supplied × supply_index`; the index clamps to `RAY/1000`, leaving S's shares with positive `unscale_supply_floor`.
3. New depositor D calls `supply` into market M; the pool credits real cash.
4. S calls `withdraw(amount = 0)` (full withdrawal). `resolve_withdrawal` returns S's floor-valued claim; `require_reserves` only checks `cash >= gross`, so S receives D's deposited tokens.
5. D's shares are now unbacked: `backing_shortfall > 0` and D cannot be made whole.

Caveat: the draining step is proven at the cache layer by `contracts/pool/tests/interest.rs:430-495`; whether the controller `withdraw` path adds a backing guard on top of `require_reserves` was not fully verified within this pass. Per INV-LIQ-04, no full-backing assertion is applied on the cleanup path itself, and `recapitalize` fills the shortfall "without restoring the lost index," which is consistent with stranded claims remaining payable.
