### Title
Attacker can front-run legitimate `Initialize2` pool creation by squatting the deterministic PDA derived from an arbitrary `market` account, permanently creating a malicious/mispriced pool - ([File: program/src/processor.rs])

### Summary
`process_initialize2` derives every pool-related account (`amm_info`, `amm_target_orders`, `amm_lp_mint`, `amm_coin_vault`, `amm_pc_vault`) deterministically from the `market` account's pubkey via a fixed PDA seed, and the `market` account itself is not validated to be a legitimate OpenBook/Serum market owned by the market program. Because the PDA is deterministic and derivable off-chain by anyone who knows (or predicts) the market pubkey, an attacker can front-run the legitimate pool creator's `Initialize2` transaction, permanently claiming the pool address for that market with attacker-chosen mints, ratios, and minimal liquidity.

### Finding Description
The seed derivation only depends on `market_account.key` and a fixed suffix, with no relation to the actual coin/pc mints supplied at initialization time and no verification that the market is owned by the OpenBook program: [1](#0-0) 

Each associated-account helper (`generate_amm_associated_spl_token`, `generate_amm_associated_spl_mint`, `generate_amm_associated_account`) only checks that the caller-supplied address matches the PDA and that it is not yet assigned; the *first* caller to submit a valid `Initialize2` for a given `market` pubkey wins the address permanently — any subsequent, legitimate `Initialize2` for the same market fails with `RepeatCreateAmm`: [2](#0-1) [3](#0-2) 

Critically, in the recommended 19-account variant of `process_initialize2`, the `market` account is explicitly documented as unvalidated ("Just a seed for AMM account. Can be any account.") — there is no check that it is owned by the OpenBook program or matches any real order book, and `amm.market_program` is simply set to `Pubkey::default()`: [4](#0-3) [5](#0-4) 

The only constraints on the initial deposit are that vault balances be non-zero and that the resulting sqrt(x*y) liquidity exceed `10^decimals` (so `user_lp_amount` doesn't underflow): [6](#0-5) 

This means an attacker who observes (or predicts, since market pubkeys are frequently deterministic/publicly created ahead of time by market makers) a `market` pubkey intended for a legitimate token pair can front-run the real project's `Initialize2` call with:
- A minimal, self-funded `init_coin_amount`/`init_pc_amount` satisfying only the non-zero/underflow constraints,
- Arbitrary coin/pc mints of the attacker's choosing (since coin/pc mints are supplied directly by the caller and only checked to differ from each other, not tied to the market),

and permanently squat the PDA that off-chain integrators, indexers, and users will compute for that `market` — since the address is deterministic and depends only on `market_account.key` and the fixed seed strings, not on the actual mints.

### Impact Explanation
Once squatted, the legitimate project can never create the intended pool at that address (`RepeatCreateAmm`), and any users/integrators who resolve "the pool for market X" via the well-known PDA derivation will instead route deposits/swaps into the attacker's malicious pool with attacker-controlled/mispriced reserves and mint composition, and the attacker holds all/most of the minted LP supply. This can result in funds lost to arbitrage against the mispriced pool, or wasted deposits into a pool that is not backed by the intended token, while also creating a permanent denial-of-service on the legitimate pool's expected address. This mirrors the reported bug class of front-running a shared, predictable identifier to seed a malicious yet outwardly "valid" object before the trusted party can act, with lasting effects that cannot be corrected after the fact.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is significant: market pubkeys for OpenBook/Serum markets are public once the market account exists (or are known ahead of time by the market-creating party who often shares the pubkey with a pool-creation partner before submitting `Initialize2`), the PDA derivation logic is public code, and any account can be supplied as `market` in the 19-account path with no ownership check, so no special privilege or race against a validator is required beyond ordinary transaction ordering/front-running (a well-understood capability on Solana, e.g. via priority fees).

### Recommendation
Require the `market` account to be validated as owned by the configured `openbook_program`/market program id (or otherwise cryptographically tied to the coin/pc mints being initialized) before deriving and creating the PDA-based pool accounts, so that the pool address cannot be squatted using an arbitrary, unvalidated account, and/or incorporate the coin/pc mint pubkeys into the seed derivation so that a given market can only produce one pool per mint pair rather than an attacker-controlled first-writer-wins pool.

### Proof of Concept
1. Off-chain, compute `pool_pda = find_program_address([program_id, market_pubkey, "amm_associated_seed"], program_id)` for a `market_pubkey` that the attacker knows will soon be used by a legitimate project for `Initialize2` (e.g., observed being created, or shared publicly ahead of the real pool launch).
2. Attacker submits `Initialize2` first (with higher priority fee to win the race) supplying:
   - the same `market_info` pubkey,
   - attacker-created/controlled `amm_coin_mint`/`amm_pc_mint` (or the real mints with minimal deposit amounts),
   - minimal `init_coin_amount`/`init_pc_amount` just above the `InitLpAmountTooLess` threshold in `process_initialize2`.
3. `generate_amm_associated_account`/`generate_amm_associated_spl_mint`/`generate_amm_associated_spl_token` succeed because the target accounts are still system-owned, and the AMM is initialized to `AmmStatus::SwapOnly` (see `process_initialize2` around [7](#0-6) ).
4. The legitimate project's later `Initialize2` for the same `market_info` fails with `AmmError::RepeatCreateAmm` because the derived addresses are already assigned, permanently blocking the intended pool and leaving the attacker's malicious pool at the expected address.
