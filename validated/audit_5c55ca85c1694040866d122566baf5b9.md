## Title
Meta-transaction balance refunds are misdirected to the delegate action's `sender_id` instead of the relayer who actually funded the deposit - ([File: runtime/runtime/src/lib.rs])

## Summary
The reported bug class is: a fallback/refund mechanism assumes that an account identifier attached to a receipt (`inputs.owner`) is the party that actually owns/funded the deposit, when in fact a different party paid for it — so a refund flows to the wrong (unauthorized) beneficiary. Nearcore has a direct protocol-level analog in its **meta-transaction (NEP-366) balance-refund mechanism**, where the runtime routes failed-execution deposit refunds to the receipt's `predecessor_id`/`refund_to`, but for `DelegateAction` receipts that identity is the delegate action's `sender_id` (e.g. "Alice") even though the actual token deposit was paid for by the relayer.

## Finding Description
When a relayer submits a `SignedDelegateAction` on behalf of a user, the runtime unwraps it and creates a new action receipt whose `predecessor_id` is the delegate action's `sender_id`, while all gas and attached-deposit costs for the inner actions are charged to and paid by the relayer [1](#0-0) .

If the inner action then fails on the receiver's shard, the runtime's deposit-refund logic sends the refunded deposit to `receipt.balance_refund_receiver()`, which resolves to `refund_to` if set, otherwise falls back to `predecessor_id()` [2](#0-1) . This receiver is used directly by `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` to build the `Receipt::new_balance_refund` [3](#0-2) .

For a delegate-action-spawned receipt, `predecessor_id` is the sender (Alice) — the party the runtime treats as the entity that "owns" the receipt — not the relayer who actually funded the attached deposit. This is the exact structural analog of the external bug: the module trusted an identity field (`inputs.owner` / here `predecessor_id`) as a proxy for "who funded this operation," but that identity does not always coincide with the actual funder, because a third party (the relayer / the bridge-fallback caller) supplied the funds on the sender's behalf.

The nearcore team is explicitly aware this creates an incentive-misaligned outcome and documents it as a known limitation of the meta-transaction refund design [4](#0-3) .

## Impact Explanation
A malicious or opportunistic sender (Alice) can construct a `DelegateAction` whose inner action carries a large attached deposit and a receiver contract Alice knows (or can arrange) will reject the call — for example, a `FunctionCall` action to a contract that will panic under conditions Alice controls, or an action targeting an account/state she can manipulate to force failure. The relayer, having verified only that Alice's balance was sufficient at submission time, pays the deposit cost up front. When execution fails on the receiver's shard, the deposit refund is routed to Alice (the `predecessor_id`) rather than back to the relayer who actually supplied the funds, resulting in a direct, unauthorized transfer of value from the relayer to Alice — a token-theft/griefing vector against relayers, which the docs explicitly flag as a "financial incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached but will fail on Bob's shard" [5](#0-4) .

## Likelihood Explanation
This is reachable through fully standard, unprivileged use of the public meta-transaction feature (NEP-366): any account can act as sender of a `DelegateAction`, and any relayer that accepts and forwards such delegate actions without additional off-chain safeguards is exposed. No special node/validator/network privileges are required — only a submitted transaction wrapping a `SignedDelegateAction` with an inner action guaranteed to fail after the deposit has been attached.

## Recommendation
Since the relayer, not the sender, pays for the attached deposit of inner actions in a meta-transaction, the protocol should route balance refunds for delegate-action-spawned receipts to the relayer (the original transaction's `signer_id`) rather than to `predecessor_id`/`sender_id`, mirroring how gas refunds already correctly go to the relayer [6](#0-5) . Concretely, the receipt(s) spawned from an `apply_delegate_action` call could set `refund_to` (the field already supported by `ActionReceiptV2`/`promise_set_refund_to`, see [7](#0-6)  and [8](#0-7) ) to the relayer's account id, so `balance_refund_receiver()` resolves correctly.

## Proof of Concept
1. Relayer R receives a `SignedDelegateAction` from Alice targeting contract `Bob` with an inner `FunctionCall` action carrying a large attached deposit and calling a method Alice knows (or can trigger, e.g. via a prior state change) will panic/fail.
2. R checks Alice's on-chain balance/allowance is sufficient and wraps the delegate action into a transaction it signs and pays for; R's shard debits the deposit+gas cost from R [9](#0-8) .
3. The unwrapped action receipt is forwarded to `Bob`'s shard with `predecessor_id = Alice`, `signer_id = R` [10](#0-9) .
4. Execution on `Bob` fails; `refund_unspent_gas_and_deposits` computes `deposit_refund = total_deposit` and issues `Receipt::new_balance_refund(receipt.balance_refund_receiver(), deposit_refund)` [11](#0-10) .
5. Because `refund_to` was never set on this receipt, `balance_refund_receiver()` falls back to `predecessor_id` = Alice [2](#0-1) , so the deposit that R funded is credited to Alice's account — a net transfer of value from R to Alice with no compensating action from Alice.

## Gas refunds in meta transactions

Gas refund receipts work exactly like for normal transaction. At every step, the
difference between the pessimistic gas price and the actual gas price at that
height is computed and refunded. At the end of the last step, additionally all
remaining gas is also refunded at the original purchasing price. The gas refunds
go to the signer of the original transaction, in this case the relayer. This is
only fair, since the relayer also paid for it.

## Balance refunds in meta transactions

Unlike gas refunds, the protocol sends balance refunds to the predecessor
(a.k.a. sender) of the receipt. This makes sense, as we deposit the attached
balance to the receiver, who has to explicitly reattach a new balance to new
receipts they might spawn.

In the world of meta transactions, this assumption is also challenged. If an
inner action requires an attached balance (for example a transfer action) then
this balance is taken from the relayer.

The relayer can see what the cost will be before submitting the meta transaction
and agrees to pay for it, so nothing wrong so far. But what if the transaction
fails execution on Bob's shard? At this point, the predecessor is `Alice` and
therefore she receives the token balance refunded, not the relayer. This is
something relayer implementations must be aware of since there is a financial
incentive for Alice to submit meta transactions that have high balances attached
but will fail on Bob's shard.
