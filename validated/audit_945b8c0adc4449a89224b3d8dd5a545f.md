### Title
Unlimited router approval combined with unrestricted arbitrary calldata forwarding enables draining of ExternalActionSwap/LifiExternalAction contract balances - (File: contracts/external-actions/swaps/LifiExternalAction.sol)

### Summary
`LifiExternalAction.callRouter()` grants the fixed `router` address an unlimited (`type(uint256).max`), persistent ERC20 allowance over the `ExternalActionSwap`/`LifiExternalAction` contract's own token balance via `approveUnlimited`, and then forwards fully attacker/prover-controlled `externalActionMetadata` bytes directly into `router.call(...)` with zero selector or parameter filtering. This is the exact risk class flagged in the external report about `LSSVMPair.call()`: an unrestricted `call()` combined with a broad grant of privilege (here, unlimited approval) lets the caller invoke *any* function the target contract exposes, not just the intended "swap" logic.

### Finding Description
In `Hinkal._externalTransact` [1](#0-0) , tokens corresponding to negative `deltaAmountChanges` are transferred into the `externalActionData.externalAddress` (here, the `LifiExternalAction`/`ExternalActionSwap` contract) *before* `runAction`/`swap` executes, so the contract now holds the user's declared `inputAmount` of `inputToken`.

`ExternalActionSwap.swap()` then calls `callRouter`, which for ERC20 input tokens does: [2](#0-1) 

`approveUnlimited` sets allowance to `type(uint256).max` for `router` and only re-approves once allowance drops below half of max — i.e. it is effectively a permanent, unbounded approval: [3](#0-2) 

Crucially, `externalActionMetadata` (`circomData.externalActionData.externalActionMetadata`) is **not constrained by the ZK circuit** to encode a legitimate swap of exactly `inputAmount`→`outputToken`; it is only bound by the `calldataHash`/public-input equality that the *same* prover who crafts it also satisfies. Since the prover is an unprivileged EOA controlling both the proof and the raw calldata, they can supply *any* bytes here. Unlike `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction`, which at least blacklists two dangerous selectors before its low-level `call` [4](#0-3) , `LifiExternalAction.callRouter` applies **no filtering whatsoever** on the selector or parameters sent to `router`.

Because `router` (a real, public aggregator contract) holds unlimited `transferFrom` rights over this contract's balance, an attacker can encode calldata for any function the router exposes that pulls approved tokens from `msg.sender` (i.e. the `ExternalActionSwap` contract) and routes the output to an attacker-chosen `receiver` — a standard capability of generic swap/bridge aggregator entry points, since the caller normally supplies `fromAmount`/`receiver` fields themselves. This decouples the amount actually pulled and its destination from the `swappedAmount = getERC20OrETHBalance(outputToken) - balanceBefore` accounting [5](#0-4)  that gates fee collection and the resulting UTXO amount in `swap()` [6](#0-5) .

This breaks the balance equation the protocol relies on to mint a UTXO backed 1:1 by real transferred value: the contract's `outputToken` balance delta it measures can be made to have no relationship to the actual value pulled from its `inputToken` balance via the unlimited approval, since the router can be directed to send the swap proceeds to an address other than this contract while it still pulls (up to) the full approved balance.

### Impact Explanation
This is a High/Critical severity issue: it allows an attacker-controlled prover to move ERC20 value out of a Hinkal external-action contract that is not tied to, or bounded by, the `deltaAmountChanges` the ZK proof actually commits value transfers to. Any token balance the `ExternalActionSwap`/`LifiExternalAction` contract holds at call time (including dust or residual balances left by other users' imperfect swaps) is exposed to this unlimited approval and unrestricted forwarding, going beyond the attacker's own deposited `inputAmount`. This matches the "theft of shielded/in-flight user funds" and "moving assets not authorised by the prover-visible accounting" impact categories.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is High for a determined attacker: it only requires crafting `externalActionMetadata` calldata targeting a known public function of the (fixed, non-attacker-controlled) `router` address that pulls approved tokens and sends output elsewhere — no special access, no admin key, and no dependence on any other party's mistake. It only requires the router to expose any generically-parameterized swap/bridge entry point that accepts caller-specified `fromAmount`/`receiver`, which is standard for aggregator "generic swap" facets.

### Recommendation
Apply the same defense-in-depth already used in `EmporiumUpgradeable.runAction` — but go further than a 2-selector blacklist:
- Do not grant `type(uint256).max` allowance to the router; approve only the exact `inputAmount` needed for the current swap, and reset to zero after use.
- Validate that the decoded `externalActionMetadata` targets an allow-listed router function/selector intended for swaps, and (if possible) that the `fromAmount`/`receiver` fields encoded in it match `inputAmount` and `address(this)` respectively before forwarding the call.
- Consider requiring the router call to specify `address(this)` as the receiver, and independently re-verify `swappedAmount` bounds against `inputAmount` and known slippage limits, rather than trusting an unconstrained balance diff alone.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker deposits `inputAmount` of `inputToken` into Hinkal with a valid proof for an `externalTransact` targeting `LifiExternalAction`, so `inputAmount` of `inputToken` is transferred into the `LifiExternalAction` contract per `Hinkal._externalTransact` [7](#0-6) .
2. Attacker sets `circomData.externalActionData.externalActionMetadata` to calldata for the router's generic-swap entry point specifying: `fromToken = inputToken`, `fromAmount =` the full current balance of `inputToken` held by `LifiExternalAction` (which `approveUnlimited` has already authorized the router to pull), and `receiver = attacker's own address` for the output leg.
3. `callRouter` calls `approveUnlimited(inputToken, router)` (granting/maintaining max allowance) then `router.call(externalActionMetadata)`, which the router executes exactly as encoded, pulling `fromAmount` from `LifiExternalAction` and sending the swap proceeds to the attacker directly.
4. Back in `swap()`, `swappedAmount = getERC20OrETHBalance(outputToken) - balanceBefore` measures zero (or an unrelated value) increase for `LifiExternalAction`, since proceeds went to the attacker, not to the contract — yet the attacker has already been paid by the router, decoupling the amount pulled from the contract's own accounting/fee logic entirely.
