### Title
Attacker can permanently evict a victim app's paid-for bandwidth allowance by flooding the FIFO subscription queue with cheap purchases - ([File: modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs])

### Summary
`pallet-bandwidth`'s `Allowance` storage caps each `(app_chain, app)` bucket at `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS = 1024` entries in a FIFO `BoundedVec`. Any unprivileged caller can invoke `BandwidthManager.purchase()` on any chain and target *any other app on any chain* (the "sponsorship" model deliberately decouples payer from beneficiary). By buying the cheapest tier 1,024+ times in rapid succession for a victim `(chain, app)` pair, an attacker forces `push_subscription` to evict the victim's legitimate, still-unconsumed, higher-value subscriptions from the front of the queue — burning bandwidth the victim already paid for. This is the same bug class as the referenced OpenQ report: an unprivileged actor fills a bounded, per-key list to its capacity limit with low-value/dust entries, permanently displacing a legitimate party's funded position.

### Finding Description
`pallet-bandwidth::push_subscription` unconditionally evicts the oldest entry once a bucket reaches capacity, with no distinction between "spam" and "real" subscriptions, and no per-purchaser accounting: [1](#0-0) 

`on_accept` (the ISMP handler invoked for every `BandwidthManager.purchase()` message) calls `push_subscription` for whatever `(msg.chain, msg.app)` pair the *payer* specifies — there is no check that the payer is the app owner or that the app has opted in to being credited by third parties: [2](#0-1) 

On the EVM side, `BandwidthManager.purchase()` is fully permissionless and lets the caller name an arbitrary `app` and `chain`, independent of the caller's own identity or chain: [3](#0-2) 

This "any buyer can sponsor any app on any chain" design is explicit and documented: [4](#0-3) 

The cap itself is hard-coded at 1024 and eviction is documented as loss-bearing ("what you paid for is yours only until it evicted"): [5](#0-4) 

Because `push_subscription` evicts strictly from the front (oldest-first) once the 1024 cap is hit, an attacker who repeatedly purchases the cheapest tier for a target `(chain, app)` will, after 1024 dust purchases, start evicting the victim's own prior subscriptions — including ones bought at a higher tier with much more remaining bandwidth and a later expiry — well before that bandwidth is drained by legitimate ISMP dispatch traffic.

### Impact Explanation
This is a freezing-of-funds analog: the victim app has paid real fee-token value (via `BandwidthManager.purchase`) for a byte allowance that the `BandwidthGate` is supposed to honor for the ISMP router to accept the app's dispatches. An attacker can force that paid-for allowance out of the queue before it is consumed, at a cost proportional only to the cheapest configured tier price times ~1024 purchases — while the victim's evicted subscription may represent a much larger, more expensive purchase (e.g., `TierFour`, purchased for months). The victim's app then loses the ability to dispatch ISMP messages (the `BandwidthGate::try_consume` will reject due to insufficient/absent allowance) despite having paid for bandwidth, and the lost bytes are unrecoverable (no refund mechanism, only an audit event `SubscriptionEvicted`). This is a permanent freezing/loss of already-escrowed value for a bounded, unprivileged bandwidth purchaser — directly matching the required "concrete theft or permanent freezing of funds" bar.

### Likelihood Explanation
The attack requires no special privilege, no compromised keys, and no governance/admin access — only ordinary `purchase()` calls, exactly the "bandwidth purchaser" actor class explicitly listed as in-scope. The economic cost to the attacker is bounded by `1024 × (cheapest tier price)`, which for typical stablecoin-denominated tier pricing (as documented, e.g. 50e18 for `TierOne`) could still be substantial in absolute terms, but is fixed and attacker-controlled (they choose the cheapest configured tier and can automate purchases without waiting for confirmations across many blocks since there is no rate limit or per-source accounting other than the ISMP dispatch cost). The griefing is more attractive against high-value, low-frequency-purchasing apps (e.g., apps that buy months of `TierFour` infrequently) where the attacker's per-eviction cost is much lower than the damage inflicted. Likelihood is Medium: it requires deliberate, sustained attacker spend, but is fully permissionless, requires no race condition, and works deterministically given the FIFO-eviction logic.

### Recommendation
- Segment the `Allowance` bucket per payer/purchase-tier, or require the credited `app` to acknowledge/opt-in before third-party sponsorship is applied to its bucket, mirroring the OpenQ fix of protecting the legitimate asset from displacement by unrelated funding.
- Alternatively, do not silently evict on overflow: reject `push_subscription` when the bucket is full (return an error/emit a distinguishable failure) rather than evicting oldest entries, or evict based on remaining value (e.g., evict emptiest/soonest-expiring subscriptions) rather than strict insertion order, so a flood of dust purchases cannot displace a large legitimate purchase.
- Consider a minimum tier-price floor and/or increasing `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS`, combined with rate-limiting purchases per `(payer, app_chain, app)` triple, to raise the cost of a queue-flood attack.

### Proof of Concept
1. Governance configures at least one cheap tier (e.g., `TierOne`) with a small `bytes`/`duration_secs` and a low `tierPrice` on `BandwidthManager` for some source chain.
2. Victim app `V` on chain `C` legitimately purchases a large tier (e.g., `TierFour`, several months) via `BandwidthManager.purchase(app=V, tier=4, months=N, chain=C)`, crediting a `Subscription` with large `remaining_bytes` and a long `expires_at` into `Allowance[C][V]`.
3. Attacker, needing no special role, calls `BandwidthManager.purchase(app=V, tier=1, months=1, chain=C)` 1024 times (any source chain works due to sponsorship semantics: `chain` need not match the attacker's chain) — see `push_subscription`'s unconditional evict-oldest-on-overflow logic: [6](#0-5) 
4. Once `Allowance[C][V].len() == MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS (1024)`, subsequent attacker purchases evict `V`'s original large subscription from the front of the FIFO list, emitting `SubscriptionEvicted { lost_bytes: <V's large remaining balance> }`.
5. `V`'s ISMP dispatches against chain `C` now fail via `BandwidthGate::try_consume` (`NoAllowance`/`Insufficient`) despite `V` having paid for the bandwidth, and the paid value cannot be recovered on-chain.

Note: I was unable to fully verify whether any additional off-chain/relayer-level rate limiting exists that would raise the practical cost of executing 1024 rapid purchases, since that would live outside the indexed pallet/contract code; this should be confirmed in a live/dry-run session before treating the exploit cost estimate as final.

		/// Append a fresh subscription with a fixed expiry. If the list
		/// is already at `MaxSubscriptions`, evict the oldest entry and
		/// emit [`Event::SubscriptionEvicted`] so the lost bytes are
		/// auditable. Returns the new subscription's `expires_at`.
		fn push_subscription(
			app_chain: &StateMachine,
			app: &AppKey,
			tier: TierIndex,
			bytes: BandwidthBytes,
			duration_secs: u64,
		) -> u64 {
			let now = <T as pallet_ismp::Config>::TimestampProvider::now().as_secs();
			let expires_at = now.saturating_add(duration_secs);
			let new_sub =
				Subscription { tier, remaining_bytes: bytes, expires_at, purchased_at: now };

			let evicted = Allowance::<T>::mutate(app_chain, app, |list| {
				let evicted = if list.len() == MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS as usize {
					Some(list.remove(0))
				} else {
					None
				};
				// Capacity is now guaranteed; try_push can't fail.
				let _ = list.try_push(new_sub);
				evicted
			});

			if let Some(old) = evicted {
				Self::deposit_event(Event::SubscriptionEvicted {
					app_chain: *app_chain,
					app: app.clone(),
					tier: old.tier,
					lost_bytes: old.remaining_bytes,
				});
			}

			expires_at
		}

	impl<T: Config> IsmpModule for Pallet<T> {
		fn on_accept(&self, request: PostRequest) -> Result<Weight, anyhow::Error> {
			let manager = BandwidthManager::<T>::get(&request.source).ok_or_else(|| {
				anyhow::anyhow!(format!("no bandwidth manager registered for {:?}", request.source))
			})?;

			if request.from != manager.0.to_vec() {
				return Err(anyhow::anyhow!(format!(
					"purchase from unauthorised sender on {:?}: expected {:x?}, got {:x?}",
					request.source, manager.0, request.from
				)));
			}

			let msg = PurchaseMessage::try_from(request.body.as_slice())?;
			let tier = TierIndex::try_from(msg.tier)
				.map_err(|_| anyhow::anyhow!(format!("unknown tier discriminant {}", msg.tier)))?;
			let cfg = Tiers::<T>::get(tier)
				.ok_or_else(|| anyhow::anyhow!(format!("tier {:?} is not configured", tier)))?;

			let bytes = cfg.bytes.saturating_mul(msg.months as u128);
			let duration = cfg.duration_secs.saturating_mul(msg.months as u64);

			let key = AppKey::truncate_from(msg.app);
			let expires_at = Self::push_subscription(&msg.chain, &key, tier, bytes, duration);

			Self::deposit_event(Event::BandwidthCredited {
				app_chain: msg.chain,
				app: key,
				paid_from: request.source,
				tier,
				bytes,
				expires_at,
			});

			Ok(Weight::zero())
		}

    function purchase(bytes calldata app, uint256 tier, uint256 months, bytes calldata chain)
        external
        returns (bytes32 commitment)
    {
        if (app.length == 0 || app.length > MAX_APP_LENGTH || chain.length == 0 || months == 0) {
            revert InvalidPurchase();
        }
        uint256 price18d = tierPrice[tier];
        if (price18d == 0) revert UnknownTier();

        uint256 total18d = price18d * months;
        address feeToken = IDispatcher(_host).feeToken();
        uint8 dec = IERC20Metadata(feeToken).decimals();
        uint256 scale = 10 ** (18 - dec);
        if (total18d % scale != 0) revert PriceNotRepresentable();
        uint256 amount = total18d / scale;

        IERC20(feeToken).safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);

        BandwidthPurchaseMsg memory body = BandwidthPurchaseMsg({
            app: app,
            tier: tier,
            months: months,
            chain: chain
        });

        commitment = IDispatcher(_host).dispatch(
            DispatchPost({
                dest: IDispatcher(_host).hyperbridge(),
                to: PALLET_BANDWIDTH_MODULE_ID,
                body: abi.encode(body),
                timeout: 0,
                fee: 0,
                payer: address(this)
            })
        );

        emit BandwidthPurchased({
            payer: msg.sender,
            feeToken: feeToken,
            tier: tier,
            months: months,
            amountPaid: amount,
            app: app,
            chain: chain,
            commitment: commitment
        });
    }

## Sponsoring Another Chain

The `chain` argument is **not validated against the source chain** — see [Overview → Sponsorship](/developers/evm/bandwidth/overview#sponsorship) for the model. A buyer on Ethereum credits an app on Base by passing the credit chain id in `chain`:

```solidity
manager.purchase({
    app:    abi.encodePacked(appAddressOnBase),
    tier:   2,
    months: 6,
    chain:  bytes("EVM-8453")
});
```

The pallet keys allowance storage on `(msg.chain, msg.app)`, so the credit lands on Base regardless of which chain sent the payment. The recommended pattern for teams running a central treasury is to deploy `BandwidthManager` on one low-fee chain and sponsor bandwidth for app instances elsewhere.
```
