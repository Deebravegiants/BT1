### Title
Hardcoded 1024-entry FIFO cap on `pallet-bandwidth` subscriptions allows a griefer to evict a victim app's paid bandwidth via cheap flood purchases - (File: `modules/pallets/bandwidth/src/lib.rs`)

### Summary
`pallet-bandwidth` stores prepaid bandwidth allowances per `(app_chain, app)` in a `BoundedVec` capped at `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS = 1024`. When the cap is reached, the *oldest* subscription is silently evicted to make room for a new one, regardless of how much unconsumed, still-valid bandwidth it holds. Because purchases are permissionless and the `chain`/`app` target of a purchase is attacker-controlled ("sponsorship"), anyone can flood a victim's `(app_chain, app)` bucket with 1024 cheap purchases to evict the victim's larger, still-live subscription, permanently destroying the bytes it paid for. This mirrors the reported bug class: a fixed numeric cap on a resource (NFTs / tiers there, subscription slots here) that a legitimate/attacker-driven configuration can exceed, with real fund-loss consequences.

### Finding Description
`MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS` is a hardcoded, non-configurable constant: [1](#0-0) 

Every accepted purchase message calls `push_subscription`, which evicts the head of the FIFO list once it hits the cap - with no check on whether the evicted entry still has `remaining_bytes` or unexpired time left: [2](#0-1) 

The purchase path (`on_accept`) accepts any inbound POST from the registered `BandwidthManager` on a source chain, and the target `(app_chain, app)` of the credit is taken from the *message body*, not from `request.source` - i.e. any caller of `BandwidthManager.purchase()` on any registered EVM chain can choose an arbitrary victim `app_chain`/`app` to credit: [3](#0-2) 

This "sponsorship" property is explicitly documented as intentional (a payer on one chain can credit any app on any chain): [4](#0-3) 

and the docs themselves acknowledge the eviction risk, only dismissing it as impractical "under pathological repeat-buy behavior": [5](#0-4) 

Because the purchase target is attacker-chosen and the cap is a fixed, ungoverned 1024 regardless of tier size or the number of distinct payers using a bucket, an attacker does not need to compromise the victim app at all: they only need to submit 1024 cheap purchases (any configured tier, `months = 1`) addressed at the victim's `(app_chain, app)` pair to push the victim's legitimate, still-live, larger subscription out of the FIFO window before it is consumed.

### Impact Explanation
A successful flood permanently destroys bandwidth the victim already paid for: `SubscriptionEvicted` fires with `lost_bytes` equal to whatever remained in the evicted entry, and there is no way to recover it - the loss is by design ("what you paid for is yours only until it expires" / eviction). For an app that pre-purchased a large-tier, long-duration subscription (e.g. the $1000/8MB tier) to guarantee message throughput, an attacker can grief it out of existence for a small fraction of that cost by buying 1024 minimal TierOne subscriptions against the same `(app_chain, app)` key, since the eviction is oldest-first and indifferent to subscription size. Once evicted, the app's `BandwidthGate::try_consume` sees no allowance and starts rejecting the app's genuine ISMP dispatches (`GateError::NoAllowance`), which is a concrete freezing/loss of prepaid funds and a denial of the app's message-dispatch capability.

### Likelihood Explanation
The attack requires only unprivileged, permissionless calls to `BandwidthManager.purchase()` (any address, any amount of times) — no special role or governance access. The cost to the attacker scales with the price of whatever tier they choose (they can pick the cheapest configured tier and `months = 1`) times 1024 purchases, which for many tier-pricing configurations (e.g. the documented $50/100KB entry tier) can be substantially cheaper than the value of a high-tier, multi-month victim subscription they aim to evict. The cap is also universal (1024 for *every* `(chain, app)` bucket, not scaled to legitimate multi-payer traffic volume for popular apps), making it plausible that even organic purchase volume from multiple sponsors targeting a popular app could unintentionally trigger evictions, and trivial for a deliberate griefer.

### Recommendation
- Make the per-`(chain, app)` subscription cap configurable by governance (mirroring the existing pattern used for `StateMachineCommitmentCap` in `pallet-ismp`), so high-traffic or high-value apps can be given headroom.
- Consider rejecting/queueing new purchases (rather than silently evicting) when the evicted entry still has significant `remaining_bytes` and time-to-expiry, or merge/compact entries instead of a rigid FIFO drop.
- Consider rate-limiting or requiring a minimum purchase size per `(chain, app)` bucket to raise the cost of a flood-eviction attack, and/or partitioning the bucket by payer so a single attacker cannot unilaterally evict subscriptions funded by other payers.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker identifies a victim app on `app_chain = X` that has purchased a large-tier, long-duration bandwidth subscription (verifiable via `Pallet::allowances(app_chain, app)`).
2. Attacker calls `BandwidthManager.purchase()` on any registered EVM source chain 1024 times, each time setting `app = victim_app`, `chain = X`, `tier = cheapest configured tier`, `months = 1`.
3. Each purchase dispatches an ISMP POST that `pallet-bandwidth::on_accept` processes via `push_subscription`, appending to `Allowance[X][victim_app]`.
4. Once the list reaches `MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS = 1024`, each subsequent attacker purchase evicts the oldest entry — eventually the victim's original large subscription is evicted and `SubscriptionEvicted{lost_bytes}` fires for it.
5. The victim's genuine ISMP dispatches now fail the `BandwidthGate::try_consume` check with `GateError::NoAllowance`, even though they paid for and expected remaining bytes/duration.

```rust
		/// Append a fresh subscription with a fixed expiry. If the list
		/// is already at `MaxSubscriptions`, evict the oldest entry and
		/// emit [`Event::SubscriptionEvicted`] so the lost bytes are
		/// auditable. Returns the new subscription's `expires_at`.
		fn push_subscription(
			app_chain: &StateMachine,
			app: &AppKey,
			tier: TierIndex,
			bytes: BandwidthBytes,
			duration_secs: u64,
		) -> u64 {
			let now = <T as pallet_ismp::Config>::TimestampProvider::now().as_secs();
			let expires_at = now.saturating_add(duration_secs);
			let new_sub =
				Subscription { tier, remaining_bytes: bytes, expires_at, purchased_at: now };

			let evicted = Allowance::<T>::mutate(app_chain, app, |list| {
				let evicted = if list.len() == MAX_SUBSCRIPTIONS as usize {
					Some(list.remove(0))
				} else {
					None
				};
				// Capacity is now guaranteed; try_push can't fail.
				let _ = list.try_push(new_sub);
				evicted
			});

			if let Some(old) = evicted {
				Self::deposit_event(Event::SubscriptionEvicted {
					app_chain: *app_chain,
					app: app.clone(),
					tier: old.tier,
					lost_bytes: old.remaining_bytes,
				});
			}

			expires_at
		}
```

```rust
		fn on_accept(&self, request: PostRequest) -> Result<Weight, anyhow::Error> {

				anyhow::anyhow!(format!("no bandwidth manager registered for {:?}", request.source))
			})?;

			if request.from != manager.0.to_vec() {
				return Err(anyhow::anyhow!(format!(
					"purchase from unauthorised sender on {:?}: expected {:x?}, got {:x?}",
					request.source, manager.0, request.from
				)));
			}

			let msg = PurchaseMessage::try_from(request.body.as_slice())?;
			let tier = TierIndex::try_from(msg.tier)
				.map_err(|_| anyhow::anyhow!(format!("unknown tier discriminant {}", msg.tier)))?;
			let cfg = Tiers::<T>::get(tier)
				.ok_or_else(|| anyhow::anyhow!(format!("tier {:?} is not configured", tier)))?;

			let bytes = cfg.bytes.saturating_mul(msg.months as u128);
			let duration = cfg.duration_secs.saturating_mul(msg.months as u64);

			let key = AppKey::truncate_from(msg.app);
			let expires_at = Self::push_subscription(&msg.chain, &key, tier, bytes, duration);

			Self::deposit_event(Event::BandwidthCredited {
				app_chain: msg.chain,
				app: key,
				paid_from: request.source,
				tier,
				bytes,
				expires_at,
			});

			Ok(Weight::zero())
		}
```

```text
### Eviction

Pushing onto a full list (1024 entries) evicts the **oldest** entry and emits `SubscriptionEvicted` with the lost bytes so the loss is auditable on-chain. In practice this only happens under pathological repeat-buy behavior — at the default of one purchase per cycle, 1024 buys is years of headroom.
```

```text
### Purchase messages skip the gate

The router uses `Pallet::is_purchase_message(request)` to identify a purchase from a registered manager (`request.source` is managed _and_ `request.from` matches the registered manager address). Purchase messages bypass the gate — otherwise a depleted app couldn't ever recharge.

## Sponsorship

The purchase message carries its own `chain` (the _credit chain_) which is **independent of the source chain** that sent the message. This means a buyer on Ethereum can credit an app on Base by dispatching a purchase whose payload sets `chain = "EVM-8453"`.

The pallet keys allowance storage by `(app_chain, app)` taken from the message body, not by `request.source`. The event `BandwidthCredited` carries both — `app_chain` (where the credit lands) and `paid_from` (where the payment came from) — so the cross-chain payer is auditable.

This is what makes the system multi-tenant friendly: a treasury on a single chain can sponsor bandwidth for an app deployed across many chains, without having to deploy `BandwidthManager` on each chain the app lives on.
```
