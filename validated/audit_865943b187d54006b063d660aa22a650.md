### Title
Unpermissioned `FunctionCall` contract-reward mechanism lets an attacker-owned contract redirect gas-fee rewards intended for a genuine application, enabling relayer/faucet draining - (File: `runtime/runtime/src/lib.rs`)

### Summary
Nearcore's runtime pays a fixed fraction (`burnt_gas_reward`, mainnet 30%) of the gas burnt during `FunctionCall` execution back to whichever account is the *receiver* of the receipt, with no permissioning or usage-based eligibility check [1](#0-0) . Any account can be the receiver of an arbitrary `FunctionCall` receipt — including one that another party (a relayer, an application, or a meta-transaction sponsor) is paying gas for — so an attacker can deploy a trivial contract, get a victim payer to call it (directly or transitively, e.g. via a free meta-tx relayer), and collect the reward instead of the real application. This is structurally the same bug class as the Canto Turnstile finding: fee/reward eligibility is tied to "whoever is invoked" rather than to any vetted, usage-qualified recipient, so it is permissionlessly redirectable.

### Finding Description
The contract-reward calculation in `Runtime::apply_action_receipt` computes `receiver_gas_reward` as a fixed fraction of `gas_burnt_for_function_call` and unconditionally credits it to `account_id` (the receipt's receiver) as long as that account still exists at the end of execution [2](#0-1) . There is no check that the receiving account is the "real" application intended by the payer of gas, nor any cap tying the reward to prior legitimate usage — exactly the class of gap the Canto report calls out ("distribute fees according to each contract's gas usage, or make the feature permissioned").

Nearcore's own architecture docs acknowledge this exact risk: "downsides, such as when implementing a free meta-transaction relayer one has to be careful not to be susceptible to faucet-draining attacks where an attacker extracts funds from the relayer by making calls to a contract they own" [3](#0-2) . Concretely: a relayer (or any party sponsoring gas, e.g. via NEP-366 meta-transactions where "all gas and balance costs ... are subtracted from the relayer" [4](#0-3) ) can be induced to call an attacker-deployed contract. The attacker's contract burns gas during its `FunctionCall` execution and receives 30% of that burnt gas back as balance, funded from the relayer's/payer's tokens, exactly mirroring the Canto Turnstile proxy-registration exploit where fees are redirected to a contract the caller controls instead of the intended application.

The protocol team has independently recognized the design flaw is serious enough to remove the mechanism entirely: protocol version 87 sets `burnt_gas_reward` from `3/10` to `0/1` under the comment "Remove gas rewards: stop paying part of the gas burned by a `FunctionCall` back to the contract account as a reward" [5](#0-4) , gated by the `RemoveGasRewards` protocol feature and validated in tests confirming the reward becomes zero once enabled [6](#0-5) .

### Impact Explanation
At the current stable protocol version (86) in this tree, `burnt_gas_reward` is still active at 3/10 [7](#0-6) . Any party that pays gas on behalf of another account being called (relayers, meta-transaction sponsors, faucets) can have funds silently diverted to an attacker-controlled contract merely by that contract being the receipt receiver. This is a concrete, unauthorized balance transfer from the gas-paying party to an attacker, matching the "concrete token... theft, unauthorized state or balance change" criterion. It is a live, reachable path via ordinary `FunctionCall`/meta-transaction submission — no validator or node-privileged access required.

### Likelihood Explanation
Likelihood is high in any scenario where a third party pays gas for calls whose target contract it does not fully control or vet — this is the explicit "free meta-transaction relayer" scenario nearcore's own docs warn about [3](#0-2) . Exploitation requires only deploying a trivial contract and inducing/using a gas-sponsoring relayer to call it; no special privileges are needed. The fact that the protocol team scheduled full removal of the reward (`RemoveGasRewards`, PV 87) further indicates this is considered a real, exploitable design defect rather than a theoretical concern.

### Recommendation
This mirrors Canto's own acknowledged mitigation path: either (a) accelerate/backport the `RemoveGasRewards` change (already implemented for PV 87) to eliminate the redirectable incentive entirely, or (b) if the reward is retained on any live network prior to that upgrade, make gas-sponsoring flows (e.g. relayers, meta-tx infrastructure) responsible for allow-listing receiver contracts, since the protocol itself provides no way to distinguish a "real" application contract from an attacker-owned one at the receipt-execution level.

### Proof of Concept
1. Attacker deploys `EvilContract` with a trivial method that burns some gas (e.g. a loop or cheap computation) under `attached_gas`.
2. A relayer/sponsor is induced to submit (or is tricked into sponsoring, e.g. via a meta-transaction per `docs/architecture/how/meta-tx.md`) a `FunctionCall` receipt whose receiver is `EvilContract`, with the relayer as gas payer.
3. During `apply_action_receipt`, `gas_burnt_for_function_call` is computed for `EvilContract`'s execution, and `receiver_gas_reward = gas_burnt_for_function_call * 3/10` is credited to `EvilContract`'s balance, debited from the relayer-funded `tx_burnt_amount` [2](#0-1) .
4. Repeated calls drain value from the relayer/sponsor to the attacker's contract, with no permission check ever applied to whether `EvilContract` is a legitimate recipient.
