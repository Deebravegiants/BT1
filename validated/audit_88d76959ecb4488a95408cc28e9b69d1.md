### Title
Emporium ops execution not bound to the shielded output recipient, allowing signature hijacking to steal a `stack.signerAddress` wallet's funds - (File: contracts/external-actions/emporium/upgradeable/EmporiumUpgradeable.sol)

### Summary
`EmporiumUpgradeable.verifyWallet` only checks the EIP-712 ECDSA signature over `emporiumMessage`, the hash of `stack.ops`, `maxFee`, and `deadline` — it never ties `stack.signerAddress`'s authorization to the `spendingPublicKey`/output recipient that will receive the resulting shielded UTXO. Separately, the EdDSA `signedMessageHash` verified in `SignatureVerifier.circom` against the *spender's own* `spendingPublicKey` is fully attacker-controlled data (the attacker builds their own `CircomData`, including `externalActionData.externalActionMetadata`), so nothing forces the two authorities to be the same principal.

### Finding Description
The claimed equality — `spendingPublicKey` (authorizing the shielded value being spent, checked in `circuits/SignatureVerifier.circom:20-27`) == `stack.signerAddress` (authorizing the `ops`, checked in `EmporiumUpgradeable.verifyWallet`, `contracts/external-actions/emporium/upgradeable/EmporiumUpgradeable.sol:302-349`) — is never enforced anywhere in the codebase.

Trace:
- `verifyWallet` recovers `recoveredAddress` from `(v,r,s)` over a hash of `(emporiumMessage, hash(ops), maxFee, deadline)` only [1](#0-0) . It does not incorporate `circomData.stealthAddressStructure`, `outPublicKeys`, `outCommitments`, or `spendingPublicKey` into what `stack.signerAddress` signs.
- `runAction` decodes `EmporiumStack` purely from `circomData.externalActionData.externalActionMetadata`, which is data the transaction submitter (any unprivileged prover) fully controls [2](#0-1) . Whoever crafts `CircomData` can embed *any* previously observed, validly-signed `EmporiumStack` (e.g., harvested from a public mempool or an unmined broadcasted transaction) alongside their own proof.
- For `invokeWallet` ops, execution runs `IHinkalWallet(stack.signerAddress).callHinkalWallet(...)`, i.e., against the *signer's own wallet*, using the signer's own funds [3](#0-2) . If those ops move assets from the signer's wallet into the Emporium contract (the normal "withdraw into shielded balance" flow the signer intended), `balanceChange` for the Emporium contract increases.
- That balance increase is then converted into a shielded output via `handleOut(balanceChange, circomData, i)` [4](#0-3) , where `circomData` (stealth address structure, output public keys, `outCommitments`) is entirely chosen by whoever submitted the transaction — the attacker, not the original signer.
- `getSignedMessageHash`/`formBasicInput` bind the spender's EdDSA signature (via `spendingPublicKey`) to `circomData.calldataHash`, which itself commits to `circomData.externalActionData` (i.e., the harvested `EmporiumStack` bytes) [5](#0-4) [6](#0-5) . This only proves the attacker's proof is internally self-consistent with whatever `externalActionMetadata` they chose to include — it does nothing to prevent the attacker from choosing someone else's signed stack, nor does it bind the stack to the attacker's own identity in any protective way.
- `HinkalHelper.performHinkalChecks` re-derives `getHashedCalldata` and checks equality against `circomData.calldataHash` [7](#0-6) , but this is an internal-consistency check, not an authorization-consistency check between `stack.signerAddress` and `spendingPublicKey`.

Root cause: the EIP-712 `EmporiumSignature` typehash (`message, ops, maxFee, deadline`) never includes any binding to the identity/output that will receive value moved by the ops, so a signature authorizing "move my wallet's funds into Emporium" can be replayed by anyone who possesses it, redirecting the resulting shielded output to themselves.

### Impact Explanation
If a signer's `EmporiumStack` authorizes ops that transfer their wallet's assets into the Emporium contract (the standard self-withdraw-into-shielded-pool pattern), an attacker who observes this signed payload (e.g., in a public mempool before it lands, or via any non-privileged channel) can wrap it in their own `CircomData`/proof and steal the resulting shielded UTXO for themselves, since `handleOut` credits the balance increase to whatever output keys the *submitter* of the transaction specifies, not the original signer. This is direct theft of a victim's funds without their consent, matching the Critical severity bar ("moving assets a wallet owner ... never authorised" / direct theft of funds).

### Likelihood Explanation
Preconditions: the attacker needs a validly ECDSA-signed `EmporiumStack` whose `ops` cause a net inflow of assets into the Emporium contract when executed against `stack.signerAddress`'s wallet, and the corresponding `emporiumMessage` must not yet be marked used in `usedMessages`. Obtaining such a signed payload requires observing it before it's consumed (e.g., front-running a pending transaction, or intercepting an off-chain-signed payload before submission) — this does not require any privileged role, compromised relay, or victim key. The attacker then only needs to submit their own transaction (their own deposit/UTXO proof) embedding the harvested stack, which is fully within the described unprivileged attacker capabilities (crafting `CircomData`, choosing ordering). This is repeatable for every such observed signature until it is consumed or expires.

### Recommendation
Bind the EIP-712 `EmporiumSignature` (and/or `verifyWallet`) to the destination of the resulting value: include a commitment to `circomData.stealthAddressStructure` / intended output recipient (or the whole `calldataHash`) in the data hashed and signed by `stack.signerAddress`, so a signed stack can only be redeemed by the specific transaction/output it was intended for. Alternatively, require `runAction` to verify the party submitting the transaction is authorized by `stack.signerAddress` (e.g., signer-provided recipient public key checked against `circomData.outPublicKeys`) before crediting `handleOut`'s balance increase.

### Proof of Concept
Foundry test plan:
1. Deploy `Hinkal`, `HinkalHelper`, `EmporiumUpgradeable`, and a mock `IHinkalWallet` (`VictimWallet`) funded with ERC20 tokens, owned/controlled by `signerVictim`.
2. Have `signerVictim` sign an `EmporiumStack` (`v,r,s`, `ops = [{endpoint: VictimWallet, invokeWallet: true, callData: transfer(Emporium, 100e18)}]`, `maxFee`, `deadline`) intending to withdraw their own 100 tokens into their own shielded balance, with a specific `emporiumMessage` nonce.
3. Before `signerVictim`'s intended transaction (containing their own `CircomData` with their own output keys) is mined, have `attacker` (holding only their own dummy/zero-value UTXOs) construct their own `CircomData` with `externalActionData.externalActionMetadata = abi.encode(harvestedStack)`, their own `spendingPublicKey`/`eddsaSignature`/`outPublicKeys` pointing to the attacker's stealth address, and call `Hinkal` to execute the Emporium action.
4. Assert: `runAction` succeeds (`verifyWallet` passes because `stack.signerAddress == signerVictim` and the signature is valid), `VictimWallet`'s balance decreases by 100 tokens, and the resulting shielded UTXO (`outCommitments`) is decryptable/spendable only by the **attacker's** `spendingPublicKey`, not `signerVictim`'s — proving the equality `spendingPublicKey == stack.signerAddress`'s intended beneficiary never held, and value was redirected to an unauthorized party.
